Projects

Constructed a heterogeneous knowledge graph recommender from Steam game meta data and interaction data. Trained GraphSAGE and LightGCN in an ensemble on the knowledge graph to predict which games a user is likely to play next. Generated human-readable justifications for the recommendations with rule-based relational path extraction to find direct paths to the recommendations and GNNExplainer subgraph attributions to rank weights of the most important relations/edges.

Models
GraphSAGE

GraphSAGE builds a user's embedding by aggregating neighbors (games they played), and a game's embedding by aggregating its neighbors (genres, developer, and publisher) while folding in its attributes (price, review volume/ratio, estimated owners, release year). This modellearns per-relation scales, letting the model decide on relationship weights.

LightGCN

LightGCN is a far quicker and stabler counterpart to GraphSAGE. It performs 3 rounds of graph propagation with a learnable scalar per edge type, and the final embedding averaged across all 3 layers. This model isolates how much signal comes from graph connectivity alone versus content features. In the ensemble, it serves as a strong collaborative-filtering baseline while graphSAGE provides a more nuanced relational understanding.

Data

We joined the Steam-200k play interactions (we used log1p-scaled hours played on a game to measure edge weight) with FronkonGames Steam metadata by exact title match. We used this resulting dataset to create a 7-node-type heterogeneous graph (User, Game, Genre, Developer, Publisher, Category, Platform), with customized relation/edge types, and each game node carrying 5 normalized numeric features from the metadata dataset (price, review volume, pos/neg review ratio, estimated owners, release year).

Explainability
GNNExplainer

This model learns a soft importance score for every edge in a compact subgraph around the user (their played games plus the games being explained, out to genre/developer), surfacing which edges most shaped the prediction.

Relational Paths

We used rule-based relational paths to search the full knowledge graph for readable user → game paths. We then rank them by how many GNNExplainer-flagged nodes they pass through, and deduplicates by bridge entity so the 3 surfaced paths aren't repeated.

A research project detecting, measuring, and attempting to mitigate a systematic formality bias in large language model outputs. LLM assistants tend to default to a more formal, polished register than humans use in comparable conversational contexts — elevated vocabulary ("delve," "utilize," "furthermore") and structured phrasing even in casual exchanges. This project measures that gap empirically and tests whether targeted fine-tuning can close it without degrading response quality. Sampled assistant turns from LMSYS-Chat-1M (25 models, real user conversations) against three human conversational baselines — BlendedSkillTalk, Reddit (TIFU), and ELI5 — and scored every sample with two independent, corroborating formality metrics. LLM outputs came out significantly more formal than every human baseline (one-sided Mann-Whitney U, p < 0.001), and the follow-up analysis pointed at conversation topic rather than model lineage as the main driver: model families cluster in a narrow band above the human baseline, with task/general exchanges running far more formal than casual ones. Finally, tried a mitigation: LoRA fine-tuning Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct on the lowest-formality slice of the human corpora. Formality Rate fell 16.5% on held-out prompts, but with only 24 evaluation samples the drop did not reach statistical significance (paired t-test, p = 0.65) — a good example of a promising-looking effect that a proper significance test doesn't yet support.

Data

Assistant turns are sampled from LMSYS-Chat-1M (25 models, real user conversations, requires an HF token to access) and three human conversational baselines — BlendedSkillTalk (scripted dialogue), Reddit TIFU (online forum posts), and ELI5 (explanatory Q&A writing). All text is filtered to a 15–600 word range and tagged human vs. LLM before scoring.

Formality Metrics
Formality Rate (FR)

A lexicon-based metric built from a two-tier vocabulary of formal-register terms, measured in hits per 1,000 tokens. It's fast, fully interpretable, and lets you point at the exact words driving a high score, but it can only catch formality it has a word for.

RoBERTa Classifier

Fine-tuned s-nlp/roberta-base-formality-ranker — itself pre-trained on the GYAFC corpus — on the Pavlick formality dataset to produce a continuous, ML-based formality score (bert_formal_p) as a second measurement that catches stylistic formality the lexicon can't. Reaches 89.2% accuracy on a held-out test split of 500 samples.

Bias Source Analysis
Statistical Testing

A one-sided Mann-Whitney U test confirms LLM formality is significantly higher than every human baseline on both metrics (p < 0.001). A Kruskal-Wallis test across the three human baselines themselves also comes back significant — even "human conversational text" isn't a single register, which matters when picking a baseline.

Model Family & Topic

Breaking formality down by conversation type (task vs. casual vs. general) and by model family tests whether instruction-tuned models are inherently more formal than base models. Model families land within a narrow 0.82–0.89 band, all above the human ELI5 baseline — topic explains more of the spread than model lineage does.

LoRA Mitigation

Fine-tuned Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with PEFT/LoRA on the lowest-formality subset of the human corpora, aiming to shift generations toward a more conversational register. Base vs. fine-tuned outputs on held-out prompts are compared with FR delta, BERT-score delta, and ROUGE-L, plus a paired t-test for statistical significance — checking for a formality decrease without a quality decrease, not just eyeballing the average.

Mean FR fell from 6.56 to 5.48 (-16.5%) and mean BERT P(formal) fell from 0.858 to 0.795, but on the 24-prompt held-out set the paired t-test came back not significant (t = 0.46, p = 0.65).

A handwriting recognition system built as two complementary approaches, trained and evaluated on two Kaggle datasets published alongside the project — one of individually segmented letter crops, one of full handwritten word images. For character-level recognition, a ResNet18 CNN and a DeiT-Tiny vision transformer are each fine-tuned on the letter crops and blended into a weighted ensemble, testing whether a convolutional inductive bias and a self-attention architecture make genuinely different mistakes worth combining. For full words, a segmentation-free CRNN — a convolutional feature extractor feeding a bidirectional LSTM — reads a word image left to right and is trained with Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss, learning the image-to-character alignment directly from (image, label) pairs without ever isolating individual letters first.

Character Classifiers
ResNet18

ImageNet-pretrained, with the stem and first residual block frozen so early low-level filters (edges, strokes) are kept and only the deeper blocks and a new classification head are fine-tuned. Trained with Adam, a OneCycleLR schedule, and early stopping on validation accuracy. Reaches 94.69% test accuracy on its own.

DeiT-Tiny

A compact, ImageNet-pretrained vision transformer with its patch embedding and earliest transformer blocks frozen, fine-tuned with the same OneCycleLR + early-stopping recipe as ResNet18. Where the CNN builds up local receptive fields layer by layer, DeiT-Tiny's patch self-attention lets every region of the letter attend to every other region from the first layer. Reaches 94.59% test accuracy solo.

Weighted Ensemble

Predicted class probabilities from both models are blended with a weight swept on the validation split rather than fixed a priori — the search lands on 0.60 ResNet18 / 0.40 DeiT-Tiny. The blend reaches 96.13% test accuracy, beating either model alone, which confirms the two architectures' errors are genuinely complementary rather than redundant. Most of the remaining confusion sits on visually similar pairs — I/L and G/Q — that trip up both models for the same reason: they're ambiguous in handwriting, not just to one architecture.

Word Recognition — Segmentation-Free
CRNN Architecture

A CNN feature extractor collapses the word image's height by 8× while preserving its width, producing a left-to-right sequence of column feature vectors. A 2-layer bidirectional LSTM reads that sequence and a linear layer emits a per-timestep probability distribution over characters — the whole thing trained end-to-end with CTC loss, so the model learns the image-to-character alignment itself instead of needing per-character bounding boxes.

Greedy CTC Decoding

At inference, the most likely character is taken at every timestep, consecutive repeats are collapsed, and CTC blank tokens are dropped to produce the final predicted word — no beam search or language model needed to get to 85.21% exact word accuracy and a 0.040 character error rate on the test set.

A real-time territory-conquest strategy game set in Sengoku-era Japan, built in Godot 4.6 with GDScript. Cities and roads are modeled as a graph loaded from custom LevelData resources; each city earns gold and army strength over time and can be upgraded across three tiers of production, gold income, and defense, with upgrade costs that scale exponentially per tier. Units march along roads in real time and battles resolve against a defending city's army and defense multiplier, all driven by a pausable, speed-adjustable cycle clock. Also built a scripted AI opponent that maps its own frontier with breadth-first search to funnel reinforcements toward contested cities, intercept incoming attacks, and prioritize upgrades on threatened borders — plus a custom in-editor level design tool for placing cities directly on the hex grid.

World & Economy
Hex Graph Map

Cities and the roads connecting them are authored as custom LevelData resources and loaded at runtime into a navigable graph — roads carry march time and distance, and every gameplay system (movement, AI pathing, siege routing) queries the same graph rather than maintaining its own copy of the map.

City Economy

Each city accrues gold and army strength every cycle and can be upgraded across three independent tiers — production, gold income, and defense — with per-tier costs that scale exponentially, so late-game upgrades are a deliberate investment rather than a straightforward snowball.

Combat & Simulation

Units march along the road graph in real time rather than teleporting between cities, so travel time is a real tactical constraint — reinforcements have to be dispatched ahead of an attack, not after it starts. Battles resolve against the defending city's standing army multiplied by its current defense tier, and the entire simulation runs on a single pausable, speed-adjustable (0.5×–4×) cycle clock that every other system subscribes to, keeping economy ticks, unit movement, and AI decisions all in lockstep.

AI Opponent

The scripted AI doesn't hard-code target priorities — it re-derives its own frontier every decision cycle by running breadth-first search over the hex graph from its owned cities, which naturally surfaces which cities are contested border territory versus safe interior holdings. That frontier map drives three behaviors: funneling reinforcements toward contested cities, intercepting incoming attacks on threatened border cities before they land, and prioritizing upgrades on the cities most exposed to the player, all recomputed as the frontier shifts over the course of a match.

Architecture & Tooling
Signal-Driven Autoloads

Cross-system communication runs through a handful of autoload singletons — CycleClock, FactionState, LevelSelection, plus scene transition and audio managers — that broadcast Godot signals instead of systems holding direct references to each other, keeping the economy, combat, and AI code decoupled and independently testable.

Custom Level Editor

A hand-built HexLevelEditor EditorPlugin runs inside the Godot editor itself: click a hex tile in the 2D viewport to place or remove a city, and the tool writes directly into the level's LevelData resource — no separate export step or hand-edited JSON between designing a level and playing it.
